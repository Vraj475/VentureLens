TAM, SAM, SOM: The Market Sizing Framework for Startups

Market sizing is one of the most frequently misunderstood tasks in early-stage startup building. Investors and founders both cite "huge market" as a reason to build something, yet most market size estimates are either wildly optimistic or poorly calculated. The TAM-SAM-SOM framework provides a structured, three-level approach to estimating market opportunity in a defensible and investor-credible way.

Total Addressable Market (TAM)
TAM is the total revenue opportunity available if a product achieved 100% market share. It represents the maximum theoretical ceiling. TAM is most commonly calculated using a top-down approach: start with published industry reports (Statista, IBISWorld, McKinsey, NASSCOM), find the total annual spending or revenue of the industry, and cite it as TAM. Example: "The global HR software market is $22 billion annually." TAM should be genuinely large — at least $1 billion — for a venture-scale business. Anything below $500M TAM typically cannot support a VC-funded startup reaching the fund return requirements.

Serviceable Addressable Market (SAM)
SAM is the portion of TAM that your product can actually serve given your current business model, geography, language, pricing, and distribution. SAM requires filtering the TAM. Example: If TAM is the global HR software market at $22B, but you're building an HR tool specifically for Indian SMBs with fewer than 200 employees, your SAM might be the Indian SMB HR software segment worth approximately $800 million annually. SAM should be calculated using an industry sub-segment or by applying multiple filters to the TAM. Investors care more about SAM than TAM because it reflects realistic opportunity.

Serviceable Obtainable Market (SOM)
SOM is the slice of SAM you can realistically capture in the next 3-5 years given your go-to-market capacity, competition, sales cycle length, and capital. SOM is where most founders either get unrealistic (claiming 10% of a billion-dollar market in year 1) or overly conservative (claiming less than 0.1% of a tiny market). For early-stage SaaS, a reasonable SOM estimate is 1-5% of SAM in years 1-3. For well-funded startups with strong distribution, 5-15% of SAM is defensible. Example: "We will capture 3% of India's SMB HR software market, representing $24M ARR."

Bottom-Up Sizing: The More Credible Method
While top-down sizing uses industry reports, bottom-up sizing starts from unit-level assumptions. Formula: SOM = (Number of target customers) x (Annual revenue per customer). Example for a B2B SaaS: India has approximately 63 million registered MSMEs. About 7% (4.4 million) have more than 10 employees and can afford software. Of these, you target manufacturing SMBs — roughly 800,000. At 1% market penetration in year 3, you reach 8,000 customers paying ₹4,000/month = ₹384 crore ARR ($46M). This is far more defensible than quoting a global industry figure.

What Reasonable SOM Percentages Look Like
For seed-stage startups (year 1-2): 0.1% to 1% of SAM is realistic. For Series A startups (year 2-4): 1% to 5% of SAM. For scaled startups (Series B+): 5% to 20% of SAM. Any projection of capturing more than 20% of a market within 5 years requires either monopoly dynamics, network effects, or extraordinary capital deployment. Indian SaaS benchmarks: Average ARR per customer for Indian SMB-focused SaaS is ₹50,000–₹3,00,000 annually. Enterprise SaaS deals often start at ₹5 lakh–₹25 lakh per year.
